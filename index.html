Meet π

π is a circle’s circumference divided by its diameter in Euclidean geometry. It is the same constant for every circle, approximately 3.141592653589793.

Essential facts

  • π is irrational: no ratio of integers equals it exactly. Its decimal expansion never terminates or becomes periodic.
  • π is transcendental: it is not a root of any nonzero polynomial with rational coefficients. This rules out exact squaring of the circle using only a straightedge and compass.
  • 22/7 and 355/113 are useful approximations, not exact values.
  • Repeating short sequences do occur. Infinite nonperiodic digits do not prove every possible sequence occurs.
  • Whether π is normal—whether every fixed-length digit block has the expected limiting frequency—is still unproved.

Explore the mathematics at Wolfram MathWorld

A journey through history

  • Ancient civilizations: Babylonian and Egyptian mathematics used early approximations.
  • 3rd century BCE: Archimedes bounded π between 3 + 10/71 and 3 + 1/7 using polygons.
  • 5th century: Zu Chongzhi obtained the remarkably accurate approximation 355/113.
  • 1706: William Jones used π with its modern meaning; Euler later helped popularize the notation.
  • 18th century: Lambert proved π irrational.
  • 1882: Lindemann proved π transcendental.
  • Computer era: Fast series and high-precision arithmetic made millions of digits practical.

Read the history at the University of St Andrews

Where π appears

Circle circumference: C = 2πr. Circle area: A = πr². Sphere volume: V = 4πr³/3. A full turn is 2π radians.

Beyond circles, π appears in waves, Fourier analysis, probability distributions, and Euler’s identity, eiπ + 1 = 0.

How do we calculate it?

Polygons give geometric bounds; infinite series give increasingly accurate approximations. The simple series π/4 = 1 − 1/3 + 1/5 − … converges very slowly. This site’s million digits were generated with the fast Chudnovsky series and integer arithmetic, with 30 guard digits.
